# Personal site v2: three concepts

## The brief (Lincoln's words, condensed)

The site is attached to job applications; its job is to make a reviewer want to hire him. He rejected the first build ("Commit Terrain": dark spec-sheet look, 3D GitHub terrain hero, dense case studies). His reasons:

- **Overwhelming:** too much information, laid out densely.
- **Not an experience:** interacting with it isn't unique. Reference: a video of a portfolio where a swarm of colourful agent cursors builds the page live ("72 agents, 832 layers"). He wants that level of experience, "but better".
- **Aggressive dark tones:** he prefers light tones.
- **No logos:** he liked having company logos and project logos.
- **Not friendly:** the UI should be thoughtfully laid out and communicative, and make technical concepts easy for non-technical people (recruiters, hiring managers) while still impressing engineers.
- **Intelligent:** the site itself should feel smart.
- **Ideas he floated:** an animated character of himself that walks the screen (some portfolios do this); a dedicated page on how he uses AI agents called **"lstack"**, his own skill set, a spoof of **pstack** (Lauren Tan / @poteto's well-known set of agent-workflow skills: poteto-mode, how, why, swarm, arena, interrogate, unslop, …).

## Content that must be there

- **Experience** (with logos):
  - Opinion System: Full Stack Developer, 2023–present; 25k-client portal, B2B SaaS, tech lead. Logo available.
  - Core Manufacturing: Founding Frontend Engineer, 2025–26; concept to production in 6 months, generative-UI chat. **No logo on hand.**
  - Earlier, condensed: GBatteries, Trading Central, Canada Revenue Agency. Logos available.
- **Cover Me** (first project, logo available): AI Chrome extension; tailors resume + cover letter in ~20 s; 200+ users, paid plans; eval harness of 32 synthetic resumes; never invents experience.
- **Open Room** (logo available; existing pixel-art agent crew Clawd, Bit, Terminal, Block, Loop drawn in code; seven demo videos incl. a wake-word demo and a 1.5x product demo):
  - Desktop app: several named Claude Code agents you talk to by voice.
  - **Moonshine story (verified in his repo):** dictation used to show nothing until Whisper finished the whole clip. He rebuilt it to stream audio every 300 ms and re-decode every 0.7 s, so words appear and settle *while you talk*. He picked Moonshine over Whisper and two streaming models by measurement: Whisper pads every clip to 30 s, so each re-decode cost 280–860 ms; Moonshine costs ~55 ms per second of audio with the same accuracy. Whisper tiny was later kept only for wake words, where it's more accurate.
  - Also: cut 73 inherited tools → only configured ones; traced a 550 ms delay to Windows Defender.
- **Agent workflow:** tackle (one ticket → one agent in its own worktree, small context, out-of-scope work becomes new tickets) and tackle-board (orchestrator, N slots, priority inheritance). Other skills he uses: writing-tickets, review-loop, gauntlet-loop, unslop, writing-pull-requests, impeccable, design-taste-frontend, motion-video.
- **Testimonials:** three quotes.
- **GitHub contribution data:** live (he liked the "niche" heatmap idea). 2,782 contributions, quiet Nov–Feb, big spike Sep–Nov 2026.
- **Contact:** email, GitHub, LinkedIn.

## Shared rules for all three

- Light, warm palette; logos everywhere they apply.
- One idea per screen; plain language first, technical depth one tap away.
- Content is never gated behind an intro.
- Works on phones; respects reduced motion.
- Stack stays Next.js on Vercel.
- **lstack** is a separate page in every variant.

## Concept A: "Little Lincoln"

**One line:** a small pixel-art Lincoln, drawn in the same style as the Open Room crew, lives on the page and treats the real layout as a platformer level.

**The experience:**

- **Page as level:** he stands on top of cards and headings, walks along them, and jumps between them, using the elements' real positions as platforms. Resize the window and the level changes.
- **Following the visitor:** he walks to whatever you hover or scroll to and presents it with a short speech bubble in plain language. At Opinion System he holds up the logo. At Open Room he sits at a desk beside the five crew members.
- **Commit garden:** the heatmap becomes a garden. Each day is a plant whose height is that day's commits, with the October spike a tall patch. He waters it as you pass.
- **Talking to him:** click him and he offers 3–4 question chips ("What do you do?", "Explain Open Room like I'm not technical"). He answers in bubbles, with a "more detail" chip for engineers.
- **Drag and drop:** you can pick him up and drop him somewhere. He lands, dusts himself off, and walks back.

**Layout:** clean light page. Hero is a short intro plus him waving. Then four chapters: Work (two project cards with logos and the Open Room video), Experience (logo row), How I build (teaser for lstack), Contact. Each chapter is a single screen.

**lstack page:** his skills as a toolbelt. He equips each one (`/tackle` is a ticket-punch, `/review-loop` a magnifier). Each card says what the skill does in plain words and when he reaches for it, and a chain diagram shows how a ticket flows through the skills.

**Intelligence:** scripted, context-aware commentary. He reacts to time of day, to how long you've stayed, to hovering a logo, and he mentions that the garden is live data.

**Impressive to engineers:** a DOM-aware platformer with collision on live layout boxes, a sprite system drawn in code, and real-time data in the garden.

**Risks:**
- A walking character can feel gimmicky or distract from reading.
- A sprite needs a likeness, which means pixel art from Lincoln's photo.
- The character must never cover text.

## Concept B: "Ask the Crew"

**One line:** the cursor-swarm video done better. Lincoln's five agent characters are live, named cursors that answer visitors' questions by assembling the page in front of them.

**The experience:**

- **The canvas:** a light, tidy board, like a calm FigJam. On load the default board is already laid out: intro, two projects with logos, experience logos, testimonials. Content is never gated.
- **Arrival:** five named cursors (Clawd, Bit, Terminal, Block, Loop) idle on it with small labels.
- **Asking:** an "Ask about Lincoln" bar with suggested chips ("What has he shipped?", "Explain Open Room simply", "How does he use AI?", "Is he a good fit for a frontend role?").
- **What a question triggers:** an orchestrator card splits it into 2–3 tickets on a mini kanban, the visible tackle-board flow. Cursors claim tickets ("Bit claimed #2"), fly to the relevant cards, drag them into an answer lane, and write a short plain-language summary card. The whole thing runs in ~3–5 s, with a narration line ("Terminal: pulled the Open Room demo video").
- **Dismissing:** one click resets the board.

**Intelligence (real):**
- **Live answers:** a real LLM (Claude via Vercel AI Gateway) answers free-text questions.
- **Grounded:** it uses only the site's own content and returns structured output naming which cards to pull and what to write. This is generative UI, the thing he built at Core Manufacturing, demonstrated live.
- **Guardrails:** common questions are pre-computed and cached (no cost, instant). Free text is rate-limited and falls back to scripted answers.

**Layout:** the board is zoomable and pannable on desktop. On phones it becomes a vertical feed where the answer cards still assemble.

**lstack page:** the same crew run a demo ticket through his real skills step by step (writing-tickets → tackle → review-loop → PR). Each step is a card explaining it in plain words, with the technical version on expand.

**Impressive to engineers:**
- multi-agent choreography
- structured-output generative UI
- caching and cost control
- the crew are his real product's characters

**Risks:**
- A chat-like feature can read as an "AI portfolio gimmick" if the answers are bland.
- LLM cost and abuse.
- The board can feel busy unless the default state is very calm.
- It's the biggest build.

## Concept C: "Field Guide"

**One line:** a light, illustrated, editorial "Field Guide to Lincoln Laylor" where every hard idea comes with a small hands-on demo, so a non-technical reader *feels* the concept.

**The experience:** scrollytelling in short chapters. Each chapter opens with one sentence in plain language and one illustration. A global **"Plain ↔ Technical"** reading-level switch rewrites every chapter's copy in place, with a smooth text morph.

**Demos:**
- **"Talk to it" (Open Room, the Moonshine story):** press and hold, speak, and watch your words appear and settle as you talk on one side. On the other, "the old way" shows nothing until you stop, then the whole sentence at once. Runs in the browser using the browser's speech API (or Moonshine on-device via transformers.js if download size allows), with the Open Room demo video beside it.
- **"The backpack" (agents):** drag tickets into one agent's backpack; past a point it starts dropping things and making mistakes. Then switch to "one ticket each" and watch three small agents finish cleanly. This teaches context windows without the words.
- **"Tailor it" (Cover Me):** pick a job title from three options and watch a sample resume line get reworded live, with the match score rising and a "nothing invented" check.
- **"The year" (GitHub):** the contribution year as a hand-drawn landscape that grows as you scroll, with the spike annotated.

**Layout:** generous whitespace, one chapter per screen, a sticky chapter index on the left, logos as small stamps beside each chapter title. Experience is a timeline of logo stamps.

**Intelligence:**
- the reading-level switch
- demos that respond to the visitor
- a "TL;DR for recruiters" card at the top that summarises fit in three lines

**lstack page:** styled as a parody "field manual" of pstack. Each skill is a numbered entry with what it does, when to use it, and a tiny animated diagram, plus an install line for engineers.

**Impressive to engineers:** in-browser speech demo, physics-light interactive teaching demos, a reading-level system, polished scrollytelling.

**Risks:**
- Less of a single "wow" moment than A or B.
- The live speech demo depends on browser support (Safari/Firefox differ) and mic permission.
- Illustration style must be consistent.
